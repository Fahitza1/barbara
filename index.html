Understanding Credit

Credit is more than a number. Learn what a score is, how it’s built, and how credit card behavior affects your future renting and borrowing options.

What is a credit score? 300–850

A 3-digit number generated from your credit report. It tells lenders how likely you are to pay back what you borrow.

How it is used Rent, loans, insurance

Landlords, lenders, and some employers use it to judge financial responsibility.

Key score factors 35 / 30 / 15

Payment history, utilization, and length of history are the biggest building blocks.

Credit Building Blocks

Think of credit like constructing a house. You need a clear foundation, steady walls, and regular maintenance.

  • Foundation: Your credit report from bureaus like Experian, TransUnion, and Equifax.
  • Structure: Accounts that report on-time payments, such as secured cards or credit-builder loans.
  • Maintenance: Pay on time, keep balances low, and avoid too many inquiries.

What a Credit Score Really Is

The score is a summary of your credit history. It is based on:

  • Payment history (35%): On-time payments are the most important factor.
  • Credit utilization (30%): How much of your available credit you use.
  • Length of history (15%): Older accounts help your score.
  • Credit mix (10%): Different types of accounts, like cards and loans.
  • New credit (10%): Too many recent applications can lower your score.
Quick fact: Your report is the file, your score is the number. Both matter.

Safe Ways to Build Credit

For first-time borrowers, use low-risk accounts that report well.

  • Secured credit card: Deposit $300-500, use up to 30%, pay it off every month.
  • Authorized user: Join a trusted adult’s account with a strong payment history.
  • Credit-builder loan: Borrow a small amount, repay it on time, and build history.
Advice: Don’t open multiple new accounts at once. One steady account is better than many small ones.

Credit Card Payment Basics

How you use a card matters more than whether you have one.

  • Statement balance: The total amount billed at the end of the cycle.
  • Due date: Pay by this date to avoid late fees and negative reports.
  • Minimum payment: The smallest amount due, not the smartest amount to pay.
  • Best practice: Pay the full balance each month to avoid interest.
  • Utilization tip: Keep your balance under 30% of your limit, ideally under 10%.

Grow Your Score Over Time

Once you’ve established good habits, use them to expand your options.

  • Request a credit limit increase after 6-12 months of on-time payments.
  • Consider a second card only when you can manage it responsibly.
  • Use rewards cards for predictable spending, then pay each statement in full.
  • Check your report every few months and dispute any errors right away.

APR reality: A $1,000 balance at 20% APR can cost $200+ in interest per year. Paying in full avoids that charge.

Common first-timer mistake: Applying for too many cards at once. Too many hard inquiries can lower your score and make you look risky.
Helpful habit: Use one card for 1-2 regular expenses, pay it off immediately, and keep a small utilization percentage.

Student Loans Guide

Student Loans • First-time borrower

Understand the difference between federal and private loans, choose a repayment plan that fits your early-career budget, and reduce interest with smart habits.

Federal loans

Fixed rates, borrower protections, and income-driven repayment options.

Private loans

Shop for the lowest rate, understand variable terms, and compare refinance options.

Loan Basics

Federal Subsidized

  • Government pays interest while you’re in school
  • Lower rates and borrower protections
  • Available based on financial need

Federal Unsubsidized

  • Interest starts accruing immediately
  • Not based on need
  • More flexible borrowing amounts

Private Loans

  • Can have fixed or variable rates
  • Often requires a good credit score or co-signer
  • Refinance when your credit improves

Prepare Before Graduation

  • Know your balance: Log into StudentAid.gov to see federal loans and servicers
  • Start small: Paying $25-50/month on unsubsidized loans cuts future interest
  • Understand your servicer: They handle billing, not your loan terms
  • Track grace periods: Federal loans typically have 6 months after graduation
Tip: Interest capitalization means unpaid school-year interest can later be added to your principal.

Repayment Plan Comparison

Plan Monthly Best for
Standard 10-Year $250-$300 Lowest total interest
Income-Driven 10-15% of income Low starting salary
Graduated Low then rising Salary expected to grow
Extended Lowest monthly Need lower payment only

Choose the plan that fits your current paycheck, then revisit annually as income changes.

Optimize Repayment

  • Set up auto-pay for 0.25% discount on most federal loans
  • Pay extra toward the highest-rate loan first
  • Keep communications with your servicer current
  • Refinance private loans only after 6-12 months of on-time payments
  • Explore forgiveness programs if you work in qualifying public service
Example: Adding $50/month to a $25k loan can save thousands in interest.

Practical Advice

  • Use a simple budget: rent, bills, loans, savings
  • Keep an emergency fund before overpaying loans
  • Watch for servicer notices and deadlines
  • Avoid refinancing federal loans unless you lose access to protections

Start every decision with your current balance, your interest rates, and a realistic first-job budget. The best plan is the one you can stick with.

Step 1: Get Your Finances in Order (2-3 Months Before)

Hey first-timer, let's start with the basics: Your rent shouldn't eat up more than 30% of your take-home pay. Here's how to calculate it properly.

🧮 Rent Affordability Calculator

Budget Breakdown (Real Examples):

  • $3,000/month income: Max rent $900 (includes $200 utilities, $100 insurance)
  • $4,000/month income: Max rent $1,200 (comfortable with savings buffer)
  • $2,500/month income: Max rent $750 (tight budget - consider roommates)

Save for These Costs:

  • Security deposit: 1 month's rent ($750-1200 typically)
  • First month's rent: $750-1200
  • Application fees: $25-50 per person
  • Moving costs: $500-1000 (truck rental, deposits)
  • Renters insurance: $10-20/month (required by most landlords)

Build Your Application Profile:

  • Credit score target: 650+ (FICO score - check free at CreditKarma.com)
  • Income proof: Recent paystubs or job offer letter
  • Bank statements: Show 2-3 months of savings
  • References: Current landlord, employer, or professor
⚠️ First-Timer Trap: Landlords aren't flexible on income requirements. If they say "$3k/month income minimum," they mean it. Don't lie—it's lease fraud.

Scenario-Based Tips

🎓 Student Scenario: If you're graduating soon, get your job offer letter early. Many apartments accept "pending employment" with proof.

Step 2: Smart Apartment Hunting (6-8 Weeks Before)

Pro Tip: Start early! 70% of rentals are filled within 2 weeks of listing.

Best Websites (Ranked by Popularity, 2024):

  • Zillow: #1 most visited - rent estimates, filters, agent connections (free basic, premium $39/month)
  • Apartments.com: Largest database, virtual tours, verified listings (free)
  • Realtor.com: MLS access, detailed property info, mortgage calculators (free)
  • StreetEasy: Best for urban areas (NYC, etc.), neighborhood insights, no-fee listings (free)
  • HotPads: Map-based search, commute times, pet-friendly filters (free)
  • Rent.com: Cash-back rewards, landlord reviews, application tracking (free)

Research Tools:

  • Crime rates: CrimeGrade.org or local police department sites
  • Commute time: Google Maps (test rush hour times)
  • Walkability: WalkScore.com (aim for 70+ score)
  • Reviews: Yelp, Google, ApartmentRatings.com
  • Rent trends: Zillow Rent Index (check if prices are rising)
💡 First-Timer Hack: Search 6-8 weeks out. Use filters for "pet-friendly" or "furnished" if needed. Save favorites and revisit weekly.

Step 3: Tour Like a Pro (4-6 Weeks Before)

What to Check (Don't Skip This!):

  • Plumbing: Run faucets, flush toilets, check for leaks (cost: $200-500 to fix)
  • Appliances: Test stove, oven, fridge, dishwasher (ask: "Included? Who repairs?")
  • HVAC: Test heat/AC, check vents (average utility bill: $100-200/month)
  • Structure: Cracks in walls/ceiling, water stains, mold (red flag if present)
  • Doors/Windows: Locks work? Windows seal? Emergency exits clear?
  • Storage: Closets, basement access (essential for first-timers)
  • Parking: Assigned spot? Guest parking? Street parking rules?
  • Noise: Thin walls? Street noise? Construction nearby?
  • Internet: WiFi strength? Provider options? Speed test?

Questions to Ask (Print This List):

  • "What's included in rent? Water? Trash? Internet?"
  • "What's the average utility bill for this unit?"
  • "How long for maintenance response? Emergency policy?"
  • "Any planned renovations or rent increases?"
  • "Can I see the lease? Subletting policy?"
  • "Pet policy? Deposit refundable?"
  • "What's the neighborhood like? Safety concerns?"
  • "How do I pay rent? Late fees? Grace period?"
  • "Can I install shelves or make minor changes?"
🚨 Red Flags to Run From: Landlord avoids lease questions • Cash-only payments • Pressure to sign immediately • Poorly maintained common areas • No emergency contact info

📋 Tour Checklist (Check as You Go)

  • Plumbing tested
  • Appliances working
  • No visible damage
  • Utilities clarified
  • Lease terms reviewed
  • Neighborhood checked
  • Parking/storage adequate

Step 4: Submit a Strong Application (2-3 Weeks Before)

Required Documents (Gather These Now):

  • Government ID (driver's license/passport)
  • Proof of income: 2 recent paystubs or job offer
  • Bank statements: 2-3 months showing savings
  • References: Employer, previous landlord, personal
  • Credit report: Free annual from AnnualCreditReport.com

Application Strategy:

  • Apply to 3-5 places simultaneously (landlords check availability)
  • Be honest about everything - lies = automatic denial
  • If credit is low, offer higher deposit or co-signer
  • Follow up politely after 3-5 days

Rental Guarantors (What They Are & When to Use):

A rental guarantor is a third party (often a parent or relative) who agrees to pay your rent if you can't. They're like a co-signer but specifically for rentals.

  • Pros: Helps if you have low credit or income, no eviction risk to guarantor if you pay on time
  • Cons: Guarantor pays if you default, can damage their credit, costs $50-200/month in fees
  • When to Use: First-time renter with no credit history, student with part-time income, or if landlord requires it
💡 Approval Timeline: 3-7 business days. They'll run background check ($20-50), credit check ($10-20), and reference calls.
✅ First-Timer Advantage: Many complexes offer "first-time renter specials" - ask! You might get application fee waived or deposit reduced.

Step 5: Review & Sign the Lease (1-2 Weeks Before)

Key Clauses to Check:

  • Rent amount & due date: Usually 1st of month, late fees $25-50/day
  • Lease term: 12 months standard, auto-renewal clauses
  • Security deposit: Max 2 months rent in most states, held in escrow
  • Pet policy: Breed restrictions, deposit ($200-500), monthly fee
  • Termination: 30-day notice, early termination fees
  • Maintenance: Who pays for what, response times

Negotiation Tips (Be Specific & Polite):

  • "Can you waive the $500 admin fee for a first-time renter?" (Often yes - saves you money)
  • "Market rate for similar units is $1,100 - can we do $1,050?" (Research comps on Zillow first)
  • "Can utilities be included for the first month?" (Good if you're moving from far away)
  • "Can we reduce the security deposit to $800?" (If you have excellent credit/references)
  • Be polite but firm - most landlords negotiate 5-10%. Have backup options ready.

Good Clause vs. Bad Clause Examples

✅ Good: "Landlord will respond to maintenance within 24 hours for emergencies, 72 hours for non-emergencies." (Clear timeline)
❌ Bad: "Tenant responsible for all repairs over $100." (Unfair burden - major repairs are landlord's job)

For more examples, see the helpful resources below.

⚠️ Don't Sign If: Terms are unclear • You can't afford it • Early termination fees > 1 month's rent • No copies provided • Pressure tactics used

Step 6: Move-In & Know Your Rights (Move-In Day)

Move-In Inspection (Critical!):

  • Take 20+ photos/videos of every room, documenting existing damage
  • Get landlord to sign move-in checklist with you
  • Keep copies - this protects your deposit
  • Note utility meter readings

Setup Essentials:

  • Change locks if needed (ask landlord first)
  • Get emergency contact numbers in writing
  • Set up utilities in your name
  • Submit proof of renters insurance
⚠️ Know Your Rights: Landlord must provide habitable space. If heat fails in winter, you can repair and deduct from rent (check your state's laws).

🏠 First Week Tasks

  • Report any issues in writing
  • Meet neighbors
  • Update address everywhere
  • Find local grocery stores, laundromats

Know Your Renter's Rights

🏠

Right to Habitability

Your home must be safe and livable.

  • Working heat/AC, hot water, electricity
  • Safe structure, no mold, no pests
  • Functional locks and lighting

If your unit is unsafe, request repairs in writing. If ignored, you may be able to withhold rent or repair and deduct, depending on state law.

🔒

Right to Privacy

Your landlord must respect your space.

  • 24-48 hours notice before entering
  • Only emergency exceptions allow surprise entry
  • No random inspections or harassment

If your landlord enters without notice, document it and remind them of your rights in writing.

💰

Security Deposit Protection

Your deposit is not free rent.

  • Must be returned within 30-45 days
  • Must include itemized deductions
  • Normal wear-and-tear is not deductible

Ask for an escrow receipt and keep your own move-in photos.

Legal Limits You Need to Know

Security Deposit Caps

Most states cap deposits at 1-2 months' rent. Example: California allows 2 months for unfurnished units and 3 months for furnished.

Ask for the exact deposit policy in writing.

Late Fee Rules

Late fees are usually limited to 5-10% of rent or a flat amount. Excessive penalties may be illegal.

Confirm the grace period and fee amount in the lease.

Entry Notice Requirements

Landlords generally must provide 24-48 hours notice before entry, except for emergencies.

Keep copies of any written notices you receive.

Repair Timelines

Many areas require landlords to fix serious problems within 7-14 days after written notice.

Use email or certified mail for repair requests.

Eviction & Notice Rules

Non-Payment of Rent

Most states require a 3-5 day notice to pay or quit before an eviction can be filed.

Lease Violations

For rule breaches, landlords often must give a cure period of 7-14 days before eviction steps continue.

End of Lease

Notice requirements vary, but 30-60 days is common for no-cause nonrenewal.

Illegal Eviction

Lock changes, utility shutoffs, or removing belongings without court action are illegal.

How to Use These Rights

1. Document everything—photos, emails, dates, and names.
2. Send repair requests in writing and keep a copy.
3. Check local tenant law websites before withholding rent or repairing yourself.
4. Use local legal aid or HUD when a landlord violates your rights.

Normal Wear and Tear vs. Damage

Understanding the difference is crucial for your security deposit refund. Normal wear and tear are expected and should not be deducted from your deposit. This includes things like minor scuffs on walls, worn carpets, and faded paint. Damage, however, is caused by neglect or abuse and can be deducted from your deposit. Examples include large holes in walls, broken windows, and stains that cannot be removed.

Common Issues and How to Handle Them

1. Maintenance Requests

Always report maintenance issues in writing. Keep a copy of your request and any correspondence. If the issue is urgent (like no heat in winter), follow up with a phone call.

2. Security Deposit Disputes

Take photos of the unit's condition before you move out. Send a written request for your deposit return, including your new address. If you don't receive it within the legal time frame, follow up with a demand letter.

3. Eviction Notices

Never ignore an eviction notice. Respond in writing, and seek legal assistance immediately. Document all communications with your landlord.

Warning Signs of Potential Rental Scams

Be cautious of deals that seem too good to be true, landlords who pressure you to pay in cash, or those who avoid providing a written lease. Always verify the legitimacy of the rental listing and the landlord's identity. Trust your instincts—if something feels off, it probably is.

Know Your Renter's Rights

These are your legal protections. Know them, use them, protect yourself.

Legal limits matter: security deposit caps, entry notice, eviction rules, late fee maximums, and repair timelines are all enforceable by law.

Your 6 Essential Rights

1. Safe and Habitable Housing

You have the right to a rental unit that is safe, clean, and fit for human habitation. This includes proper maintenance, repairs, and compliance with health and safety codes.

2. Security Deposit Protection

Your landlord can only charge a security deposit that is equal to 1-2 months' rent, depending on state law. This deposit must be returned to you within a specific time frame after you move out, minus any deductions for damages beyond normal wear and tear.

3. Privacy and Quiet Enjoyment

You have the right to privacy in your home. Landlords must provide reasonable notice (usually 24 hours) before entering your unit, and can only enter for legitimate reasons, such as repairs or inspections.

4. Fair Treatment and Non-Discrimination

It is illegal for landlords to discriminate against you based on race, color, national origin, religion, sex, familial status, or disability. You have the right to fair and equal treatment in all aspects of the rental process.

5. Right to Organize

You have the right to join or organize tenant unions or associations. Landlords cannot retaliate against you for participating in these activities.

6. Legal Recourse

If your rights are violated, you have the right to seek legal recourse. This includes the right to sue for damages, seek injunctive relief, and recover attorney's fees in certain cases.

Additional Important Rights

7. Right to a Written Lease

You have the right to receive a written copy of your lease or rental agreement. This document should outline all terms and conditions of your tenancy, including rent amount, due date, and lease duration.

8. Right to Repairs

Your landlord is responsible for making necessary repairs to keep the property in a habitable condition. This includes fixing plumbing, heating, and electrical issues, as well as addressing pest infestations.

9. Right to Notice of Rent Increases

Landlords must provide proper notice before increasing your rent. The notice period and allowable increase amount vary by state and local laws.

10. Right to Challenge Eviction

You have the right to contest an eviction notice in court. Landlords must follow the legal eviction process, which includes providing proper notice and a valid reason for eviction.

Security Deposit Caps

Most states limit deposits to 1-2 months' rent. In California, it’s 2 months for unfurnished units and 3 months for furnished.
